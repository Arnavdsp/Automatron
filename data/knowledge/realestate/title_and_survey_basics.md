# Title commitment and survey basics

Summary written for this project. Not legal advice. Local practice varies; a licensed attorney
reviews these documents.

## What a title commitment is

A title commitment is an insurer's offer to issue a title policy on stated terms. It has
distinct parts, and confusing them is a common source of error.

Schedule A states the proposed insured, the amount, the estate being insured and the legal
description.

Schedule B-I lists requirements: things that must happen before the policy issues. A payoff and
release of an existing mortgage is the usual example. An item left unsatisfied here is a live
issue, not a historical note.

Schedule B-II lists exceptions: matters the policy will not cover. Recorded easements,
covenants, mineral reservations and survey matters commonly appear. An exception means the buyer
takes the property subject to that matter.

## Recurring exception types

Easements granting a utility or a neighbour the right to use part of the land. The location
matters as much as the existence: a ten foot utility easement along a rear boundary constrains
what can be built there.

Covenants and restrictions limiting use, often through a homeowners association.

Liens and encumbrances. A mortgage expected to be released at closing is routine; an unreleased
lien from a prior owner, a mechanic's lien, or a judgment lien is not.

Mineral or water rights severed from the surface estate.

## Survey

A survey shows boundaries, improvements, and encroachments. It is what connects a recorded
easement to a position on the ground. Common findings include a fence or structure crossing a
boundary, an improvement inside a setback, and a driveway relying on an access easement.

Where no current survey exists, the policy usually carries a general survey exception, and
matters a survey would have revealed are uninsured.

## What a red-flag memo should do

Identify each item, name the document and page, explain in plain language why it may matter for
the buyer's stated intended use, and pose the question for the attorney. It should not conclude
that an issue is fatal, that a matter is legal or compliant, or that it is safe to close.
