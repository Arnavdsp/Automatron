# Orbital debris mitigation

Summary written for this project. Verify against the official source before relying on it.

Debris mitigation guidance is broadly consistent across national and international bodies, even
though the binding requirements differ by jurisdiction. The recurring themes below appear in
most regimes.

## Limit debris released during normal operations

Designs should avoid deliberately releasing hardware such as separation devices, lens covers and
straps into orbit.

## Minimise the chance of break-up

Stored energy is the main driver of on-orbit break-ups. Passivation at end of mission — venting
propellant, discharging batteries, relieving pressure vessels — is the standard mitigation.

## Limit collision risk

Operators are expected to assess collision probability against catalogued objects, to have a
means of responding to close approaches, and for larger constellations to be able to manoeuvre.

## Post-mission disposal

Low Earth orbit objects are expected to be removed from orbit within a defined period after the
end of mission. The historical guideline was twenty-five years; several regimes have since moved
to substantially shorter periods, and the applicable rule depends on the licensing jurisdiction
and the date. Geostationary objects are expected to be raised to a disposal orbit above the
operational belt.

## Casualty risk on reentry

Where a controlled or uncontrolled reentry leaves surviving fragments, the expected casualty
figure is assessed against a threshold. Design-for-demise reduces the surviving mass.

## What this means for a decision brief

Disposal timelines and casualty thresholds vary by jurisdiction and have changed recently. A
brief should cite the rule that applies to the specific licence being sought and mark any figure
it cannot source as needing verification, rather than quoting a remembered number.
